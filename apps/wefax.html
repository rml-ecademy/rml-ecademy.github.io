    📶 CHOOSING THE FREQUENCY
    HF propagation changes with time of day and distance. Rule of thumb: daytime → higher frequency (8–17 MHz), night → lower (4–8 MHz); greater distance → higher. Too high = signal skips over you; too low = absorbed in daytime. Step through CH01–CH04, watch the S-meter and choose the strongest, clean signal. Poor signal = speckled, streaky chart.
  
    General SSB receiver? Tune USB 1.9 kHz below the assigned frequency (e.g. 8467.5 → 8465.6). This dedicated receiver applies the offset itself: enter the assigned frequency.

    WHAT IS REAL / SIMPLIFIED
    Real: HF radiofacsimile (F3C) broadcasts on a fixed UTC schedule; start signal 300 Hz (IOC 576) / 675 Hz (IOC 288), 30-s phasing, 450 Hz stop signal; 120 LPM standard; wrong LPM = slanted chart, wrong IOC = stretched / squashed chart, no phasing = chart split sideways; USB 1.9 kHz below assigned frequency on general receivers; frequency choice by time and distance; chart symbols (isobars every 4 hPa, H / L, fronts, wind barbs, [GW] / [SW] / [TW] warnings, typhoon forecast circles); Buys Ballot's law and the classic typhoon-avoidance rules for the northern hemisphere.
  
    Simplified: the station, call sign, frequencies and schedule are fictional; the weather is computer-generated for training; coastlines are rough; the panel is modelled on a typical wall-mounted weather fax recorder, but the menus are generic (real units differ by maker); propagation is a simple model. Always use real met-service charts, the equipment manual and the company SMS.
